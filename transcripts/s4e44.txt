## The creator and the critic

TOM: So I'll tell you the basics of *Super Draft*. I was in my early to mid-twenties when I sold the first script, and all my friends were film school grads who wanted to do the same thing, so we were constantly talking about this. I came to the conclusion that every human being on the planet has two voices in their head: the creator and the critic.

I've never in my life needed to explain the critic voice to anyone. They immediately say, "Oh, I know that one. I'm intimately familiar with that one." You could be a janitor in a high school and you'd know the critic voice. Mine sounded a lot like my eleventh-grade English teacher, to be honest, Mrs Bond. She had a really nasally voice.

NICK: Is that the voice you hear when you get doubts in your head? Her saying, "I knew you couldn't do it"?

TOM: For a lot of women, it's their ex-husband's voice a little bit. Whereas the creator voice is very different. It's quiet and whispering, just giving you a little hint here and a little hint there. And I came to the conclusion that you can never hear both voices in the same room. If the critic voice is operating, you cannot hear the creator. And working in a critic-only process, for a creative process, is death. It is pain. You can do it, but you're grinding the words out, just trying to get to the next sentence. Write, delete, adjust, rethink, despair, write.

DOUG: It's probably going to take you a long time to write that manuscript too.

TOM: It does. And yet so many people have that same process to this day. So I realised I needed to separate those voices, so that there's a creator-led part of the process and a critic-led part, and you go back and forth. Brainstorming is a creator-led process. It's the only time in the business world you'll ever hear any acknowledgement of a creator voice. What do they say? "There are no bad ideas here." And why do they say that? They don't believe there are no bad ideas. They're executives. They think there are horrible ideas all the time. They've just figured out that if you don't say that, nothing good happens in a brainstorm. It's literally shutting the critic out of the process, which allows the creator to come forth.

NICK: I'm a big fan of that, Tom. The business I came from was super competitive motorsport.

DOUG: It's a sport. You had to win a trophy.

NICK: It's naturally competitive, and there's a lot of ego involved. When I made it to management, one of the things I learned was that there's no bad idea, because the dumb idea you just came up with, the one with a fatal flaw in it, spurs another idea that gets rid of that flaw. Together, the room is stronger than the individual. If you can build that atmosphere, which is really hard, it only takes one personality to destroy it. So I can only imagine it being hell in Hollywood.

TOM: They know it in Hollywood. What they say to me all the time is, "Here's the bad version." It's, "I don't know how to put this. I don't like this part of the story. ## When the creator never meets the critic

DOUG: Let me ask you about the flip side. We talk about the critic voice a lot, but what I sometimes see in new authors, the people on Threads announcing their new book, is that they're stuck on the creator side and haven't bothered listening to the critic at all. Their book is the most amazing book ever, and that's what they tell everybody. We've all talked to somebody who says, "No, let me tell you my story," and we roll our eyes, but we were probably all there at some point, because all we were hearing was the creator side. You probably see that in Hollywood as well.

TOM: All the time, but let's not misdiagnose that. That's just a person who's really early on the Dunning-Kruger arc. Dunning-Kruger is very simple. When we begin a process, we overestimate how easy it's going to be, and then as we start doing it, we drop down into the trough of hell, the point where we think, my God, this is so hard, it's impossible, I can't get any better. Eventually you start coming up the other side, once you've gained enough experience to have a reasonable notion of your skill level versus how hard it actually is. Those authors are just Dunning-Kruger acolytes. They're at the beginning of that curve, and they haven't really done it yet.

Rare is the person who says, "My goodness, it's the greatest idea ever," and has actually written it, or written multiple books. It's almost always, "I want to tell you this idea because I want you to write it," or, "I'm about to write it," or, "I'm starting it." They haven't hit any of those points yet, because boy, that critic is eventually going to find you. And I don't think you can be a good writer without both.

I used to talk about the critic as the enemy of the writing process, only because more often than not the critic puts in the roadblocks and stops you from writing. But the critic, properly used, is integral to the writing process. Outlining, whether you're a plotter or a plantser, is taking all the brainstormed ideas floating around and picking winners and losers, framing and shaping them. That's absolutely a critic process. Editing is a critic process too, especially if you're free drafting, or super drafting as I call it, where you literally shut the critic out of the drafting and write as fast and as freely as you can. You're going to need to do some critic work after that. So it's a tough thing to come to terms with, but you've got to make friends with your critic, or at least...

DOUG: A little pressure. A little mix of both probably helps.

TOM: A little pressure is not a bad thing. A lot of pressure can be, and it varies by personality, but a little pressure is incentive. If you use it to drive you forward rather than push you back, it's crucial.

## What Super Draft gives an author

NICK: So tell us a little more about *Super Draft*. When's it coming out, and if I'm an author picking up a copy, what am I getting from it?

TOM: Thank you, Nick, for bringing it back to something relevant. Here we go. It should be out by the end of October. There are hundreds and hundreds of books about the craft of writing, and maybe five or six about the process of writing, which is weird to me. Why do we talk so much about craft and so little about process? At the core of it, I believe it's because we're precious about our process. We're emotional about it in a way we aren't about craft. Tell me something new about craft and I'll throw it into the next book I write and see if it sticks. Tell me you want to monkey with my process, and I get really defensive really quickly.

DOUG: I'm sorry, you have a monkey write your stuff? I missed that.

TOM: Yeah, basically. You don't have a draft monkey? You've got to get a draft monkey.

NICK: That should be the name of your next book: *Draft Monkey*.

DOUG: It's the name of our band. We're coming out with a new album, *Draft Monkeys*.

TOM: If you gave me a nameplate for my desk that said "Thomas Dean Donnelly, Draft Monkey", that would be a perfect description of what I do.

NICK: And yet another T-shirt we have to make.

TOM: Anyway, what it comes down to is that writing a novel is extraordinarily hard, and writing a screenplay is extraordinarily hard. We get used to it, so we don't think of it as quite that hard, but in our sense memory we still have that first book and the labour pains of getting it out. We tend to stick close to the first process that got that book out, because getting it out was so hard that whatever we had to do must be the way we're going to do it. We calcify that a little, and we don't explore other options.

I wrote this book first and foremost for people who haven't written their book yet, to get past the imposter syndrome and the writer's block and create a process that works for them. Whether you're a plotter or a pantser, you can use parts of this process to write faster and better, because you're using both voices in your head, not just one. Most of our processes rely very heavily on one voice and give the other short shrift. That's really what *Super Draft* is about.

## Phoebe and Philip

TOM: The critic has all of these tricks it uses to get into the drafting process. I name mine. My creator is Phoebe and my critic is Philip. And I don't actually have a friend named Philip. I don't know anybody named Philip.

DOUG: I was going to say, where did this come from?

TOM: Phoebe is from *Friends*, strumming a guitar, playing a silly song, talking to you nicely. I came up with Philip one day when I was annoyed as hell with my critic voice, which just wouldn't leave me alone, and I decided I had to give it a name. And Philip is a great name to say with derision. "Shut up, Philip. You're not welcome here right now. God, Philip, damn it."

DOUG: "Shut up, Philip" absolutely works. I have a cousin named Philip. I'd buy that shirt just to wear it around him.

TOM: You guys were at SPS Live in London this year, where my talk on *Super Draft* was the very first talk of the conference. Every time I mention Philip, there's always one person with a big frown on their face, and I think, there's a Philip over there. I know they're going to come and talk to me afterwards, and they always do: "Hi, I'm Philip." And I say, "I am so bleeding sorry. I don't mean anything bad by it. It just happens to be the name."

But naming your critic is a good thing to do, because you're externalising the voice and giving it its own personality. It's not you. It's something else that you can shut out. You have to learn to tell Philip what's okay and what's not, because Philip hits below the belt. Philip won't criticise the work, he'll criticise you, and that is not okay. I tell my kids this all the time: if I heard anyone talking to you the way you talk about yourself, I would punch that person in the face, and I'd go to prison if I had to. I would never let anyone talk to you the way you talk to yourself, so why is it okay for you to do it?

## Research, rabbit holes and writing downhill

TOM: In *Super Draft* we talk about things like research. Research can be a very important part of your writing process, and it can also be a fantastic trick the critic uses to put something between you and the actual drafting.

DOUG: You're talking about us, because that's our rabbit holes. Nick and I always spend...

NICK: ...down a rabbit hole.

TOM: Well, guess what: I've got a chapter on research and how to do it without letting it become critic-led procrastination. I also have the write-downhill protocol. When you're writing, have a timer, and when the timer goes off, stop. Even if you're not a big sprinter, because not every writer likes sprints. Don't think of it as sprinting or hitting a word count. Just stop when the timer goes off. People ask me why. Here's the deal: if you stop exactly when the timer goes off, you're usually stopping mid-sentence, when you know exactly what's coming next. You'll want to finish that word, that sentence, that paragraph, that chapter. Don't. Because if you do, every time you start writing, you're starting cold, at a place where you don't know what happens next. The first word of a writing session is often the hardest, and so are the first sentence and the first paragraph, because you're starting cold. If you know exactly what comes next, it's much easier to start.

I call it writing downhill because I had a really awful car when I moved to Los Angeles. It was a piece of junk and wouldn't start half the time. But I realised that if I parked it on a downhill slope, I could get in, do a rolling start, and I never missed a meeting again.

DOUG: For all of our younger listeners: cars used to have stick shifts. That trick won't work now, and you can't buy a stick shift any more. Even my Jeep won't do it. It's a stick shift, but they've added a safety feature, which kind of annoys me, because I've tried it a couple of times.

TOM: This car was the Ford Festiva L. I couldn't even afford the LS or the LX. I could only afford one letter. It had no air conditioning, in Los Angeles. It had no radio. It had a manual transmission, and it was a McNugget box of a piece of garbage.

DOUG: Or open like the Flintstones, where you go "brrrr" with your feet and take off.

TOM: It was almost that bad. Anyway, I have all these tricks to outwit the ways the critic tries to stop your creative process. The second half of the book goes into ways to hear your creator better. When the creator really fires, it's like a bolt of lightning: my God, where did that come from? But brain science has shown there are all sorts of things we can do to get struck by lightning more often. And as writers, wouldn't we want to be struck by lightning more often?

DOUG: That'd be nice. Well, people might need to check out *Super Draft*. You said the end of October, so about the time this comes out, it'll be live.

TOM: It'll be out, yes.

## Tom's author superpower

DOUG: We have two more questions for you, because we've kept you a while and we appreciate your time. Our first is one we ask everybody: what do you think is your author superpower?

TOM: My author superpower is actually the creator voice. People ask me, "Do you ever run out of ideas?" My God, no. I have more things to write than I could write in four lifetimes, and it's always coming up with something new every day. Over the years I've learned to train my brain to retain those ideas and hang on to them a little more. I was riding on the subway once and saw a homeless woman talking to herself, and I got the idea: what if she's not just talking to herself? What if she's hearing the thoughts of everyone in the train carriage around her and responding to them, and it's driving her crazy, because they're actual voices? That was the impetus for the first screenplay I ever wrote, *Thoughtcrimes*.

DOUG: That's a pretty good superpower.

TOM: Thank you. I totally agree, but that's not one I was born with. I had to create that one out of whole cloth, out of a lot of pain and a lot of misery.
