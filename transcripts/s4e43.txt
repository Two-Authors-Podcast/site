## Writing characters with disabilities

DOUG: So talk to us a little about the main challenges you've faced as a writer. You were already writing before the amputation, right? Playing around with it?

KATIE: Playing around with it, yes. I had three kids, so it was pretty much just for fun at that point.

DOUG: What challenges have you faced that we wouldn't think of?

KATIE: Well, I write main characters with disabilities. In the very beginning I was fighting books like *Me Before You*, books where the character who's disabled is either a poor pitiful Pearl or dies at the end, or is miraculously healed by love. That was my other favourite.

I found the need was there for the disabled community to be represented accurately in romance, but it was also still a hush-hush topic for abled people. The reason my covers always just had heads was because you couldn't find stock images of people with disabilities. So a lot of the time my books were bought and read without people realising there was a character with a disability, and I would get pushback on that. People would come at me in private messages and say, "You need to disclose this on your books," and I'm not using the words they would use. I don't get that mentality. You don't have to finish it.

So slowly I realised that the disabled community wants these books to read, but they're not my audience. I mean, they are my audience, obviously, and I love them, but my real audience is getting these books into abled readers' hands and teaching them about our community while giving them a story.

DOUG: Okay. So the disabled community was your audience, but what you wanted was to reach the able-bodied and start spreading a message.

KATIE: Correct. The disabled community loves the books. They would email me nonstop: would you consider this disability, or would you write about that disability? And over the years they've also helped me reach a lot of abled readers. I probably have as many abled readers now as disabled readers, which starts changing the mindset that people with disabilities need to be hidden away, that they can't have an epic love story, that they aren't out working and contributing to society in any positive way.

And with time, of course, time changes everything. Back then I was the only one writing romance with a main character with a disability. Now I'm in a group chat with about fourteen other women who are authors with disabilities writing main characters with disabilities. So it's been cool to see that progression of acceptance over the years, and the younger generation of authors coming up to keep filling the needs and wants of readers.

NICK: Let me ask you this. For anyone who wants to include a disabled character, what's the first thing they should do, and the first thing they should avoid doing?

KATIE: The first thing they need to do is have some kind of knowledge about it beyond Google. And it's always helpful if they have a reader or a friend they can reach out to with questions. The other thing they need to think about is the plot of the book. If this person has a particular disability, are they going to be able to do what's happening? That's a big thing for me writing suspense: will the disability I've picked carry over and allow me to do what I need to do with the plot?

DOUG: No, but wait a second. Katie, have you watched *Reacher*? There's no reason for that human to be physically able to do the things he does.

KATIE: Okay, but I say the word "accurate" a lot. Even though it's fiction, I try to make the disabilities accurate. Obviously in fantasy you don't need to do that, you can do what you want. But if you're writing any type of contemporary romance or romantic suspense set now, or even in the past, you've got to have that knowledge.

A lot of abled authors ask me what the first thing they should do is if they want to write a character with a disability, and my answer usually is: don't. Because the representation needs to come from within the community more than it needs to come from the abled community, because that's what we've always had. We've always only had books written by abled authors who get it wrong every single time.

Unless they have, for example, children with disabilities. I know a lot of authors like that, and they live that life, they live in the community, and they have a deep understanding of how to write those characters. But people who don't know anybody in the disabled community are going to struggle a lot to write a main character with a disability and get it right. I'm not saying it can't be done, but it's going to be more work than they think, between research and finding people who can help, and, like I said, not using Google.

NICK: One of my Nora Sommer books features a sight-impaired character. She's completely sight-impaired, and I knew I needed somebody. I wasn't going to rely on just putting my version of it out there. Through another author friend, I found someone who's sight-impaired. She's actually in Scotland, she taught at a university, and she's an absolutely lovely lady. I sent her the chapters as I wrote them.

The biggest thing it gave me was that, knowing I was sending it to somebody who lives with that challenge, it really made me think about the details and put myself in their shoes, literally every step of the way. I felt like that made a big difference. I felt accountable. She had some really good feedback, and fortunately not a huge amount of adjustments. Several times she said, "Wow, I'm surprised you got this right." And I feel like the only reason I did was that I had her, and I knew someone was going to call me out.

DOUG: Now, she did have notes on the rest of your book, though. She said, "You got this right, but maybe the rest is crap."

KATIE: And I think that's important too: holding yourself accountable if you're going to do it, having somebody you can send the chapters to, so that you feel confident putting it out there and that you're not doing harm to the community by writing that character. That's always in the back of my mind. I am part of the community, but I don't want to put anything out there that is somehow going to do harm to someone who picks it up, someone I don't know.

So my goal is always to make it as accurate as it can be while still making it romantic and fun, because we're all human and we all have the same human experiences, regardless of whether we walk, use a wheelchair, or talk with our hands. That's what I try to portray on the page.

## Sensitivity readers

DOUG: A lot of people talk about using sensitivity readers. Is that something you'd advise? And if so, how would you go about finding them?

KATIE: It's so easy to find sensitivity readers now, and I do recommend it for sure if you're an abled writer. It can be someone you know in the disabled community. It doesn't have to be someone you pay. It just needs to be filtered through the eyes of the community you're writing about. You can find them on Instagram, or you can Google and find them.

I've done it myself. Several authors have sent me their books and I've sent them my notes, and I've had a couple who refused to release the book because it was so wrong. They said, "I can't in good conscience release it this way." One in particular was an amputee book, and they just didn't realise all the things they got wrong, because they didn't know anyone who was an amputee. They wrote it from their perspective of what they thought an amputee would do, say and go through, and it just didn't work.

But I do recommend it. It's much easier these days than it used to be, because there are a lot more connected communities for writers.

## Books Breaking Barriers

DOUG: Let's talk about your advocacy programmes. Tell us a little about Books Breaking Barriers.

KATIE: Books Breaking Barriers is designed to do what I talked about before: getting books with disabilities into the hands of abled readers in a way that isn't "scary", and I'm putting scary in quotation marks. They find a book they can enjoy and talk about on social media, and spread the word that not just me, but all the authors with disabilities writing these books, are sharing their truth about their community. These books have changed so much since *Me Before You* and the books that used to have characters who were disposable, which is the best word I can use. And there's nothing to be afraid of in these books. There's nothing gross. They're no different from any other romance you'd pick up off the shelf.

So I started the initiative with a lot of information, teaching people-first language. All the information they need goes into the package, so that when it arrives at their door they feel confident not just reading the book, but talking about the book with others, which is very important.

Any influencers can go on my website and send me a message. I have two options, either a romantic suspense or a contemporary romance, that I include in the package, and then they have the opportunity to read it.

NICK: When I was a kid I didn't eat well. I still don't eat vegetables. My mum used to cut my carrots up really small and hide them in the mashed potato, thinking I'd deal with them if they were hidden in there. In book terms, is hiding the disability behind something else, to make able-bodied readers more comfortable as an entry point into that world, something you consider? Or do you stay away from that, and it's basically: here it is, take it or leave it?

KATIE: That is pretty much my go-to. I'm not ashamed of being disabled, and I don't think we need to hide it. But I have done two series where the character with a disability is a secondary character in the first book. For instance, in *Cupcake* neither main character has a disability, but her best friend is disabled. Then the second book is *Tart*, and it's about her. By then readers are already hooked because they read *Cupcake*. I've done that a couple of times, and it has been successful in drawing people into the second book, because they already know the character from the first and they're no longer trepidatious about it.

That works with my contemporary romances. With the romantic suspense for Harlequin, every book has a character with a disability, because even when they're part of a series, they're standalones, so it doesn't work as well there.

I decided I was going to submit a book to them. I'd always written my indie books in first person, and they don't take first person, it has to be third. I really wasn't comfortable writing in third person, but slowly I wrote a couple and thought, okay, I can do this.

I wrote the first one, *Going Rogue in Red Rye County*, in about five days in the middle of COVID, when I was taking my [unclear]-year-old son to college for the first time and leaving him there in the middle of COVID. So I escaped into this other world when I could to write the story.

I submitted it through a Twitter pitch Harlequin ran at the time, Romance Includes You, which interestingly has become an actual scholarship now, so I'm pretty excited about that. You pitched your story to an editor in about 140 characters, and you also had to use that long hashtag, so you didn't have much room. If you got a like, you sent in your whole manuscript. So I got a like and I sent it in.

And then it got lost in their system. It was about five months later that I finally reached out to one of the concierge ladies, and she was able to find the manuscript and get it into an editor's hands. KATIE: There are days like that too. But generally I try to edit a different book in between, so that I forget those characters and everything that happened, and when I come back to it I have fresh eyes, which helps a lot. Then it might take me another week to two weeks to edit it to the point where I feel I can send it to my editor at Harlequin. They send me back the things I obviously missed, I fix those and send it back, and then it goes to the copy editor. So it's a process.

But I always feel that at the end of the process the book is the best it can be, telling the story in an accurate way for readers with disabilities and also engaging abled readers to want to pick up the next book in the series. That's always my goal.

## Disability on the cover, and on the shelf

KATIE: Harlequin has been wonderful at finding stock for covers that represent the disabilities, which is not easy to do. So it has been nice to see these books on the shelf in Walmarts across the country, with characters with disabilities on the cover.

DOUG: I was going to ask about that, because I was just in a Walmart and saw they had that whole Harlequin wall, and some of them featured disabilities.

KATIE: Harlequin has worked really hard to develop shelf space with Walmart that is inclusive and holds a selection of all their books every month, which hasn't always been the case. He's a hemipelvectomy amputee and doesn't use a prosthetic, and he's in his wheelchair on the cover. You guys can see it, even though no one else can. I feel that matters, not just for the curiosity of abled readers who pull it down, but for disabled readers to see it.

DOUG: I was actually surprised, because Walmart doesn't have a huge book selection. It used to be a long row and now it's shrunk down. But my wife and I were both surprised and pleased to see so much representation on the shelf.

KATIE: Yes, they've worked really hard. They've changed how they work with Walmart. They've cut out the middleman who used to stock the books, and now Walmart does it. There have been some hiccups, but overall it's become a better situation for readers.
